🎯 TODAY'S FOCUS

The allies who defeated Hitler were soon rivals who feared each other. Today you will see how Europe was divided, how the United States and the Soviet Union came to face each other with atomic weapons, and how close that rivalry came to open war.

ALLIES BECOME RIVALS

The alliance of the United States, Britain, and the Soviet Union had been held together by one enemy. In February 1945, with the Red Army only a few dozen miles from Berlin, Roosevelt, Churchill, and Stalin met at Yalta in the Crimea. They agreed to divide Germany into zones of occupation and to hold free elections in the liberated countries of Eastern Europe. Stalin signed, but he had no intention of allowing free choice in lands his army had taken at so high a price. He wanted a ring of friendly states, for Russia had been invaded from the west three times in a century, and he trusted only governments he controlled.

Roosevelt, ill and tired, died on April 12, 1945, only two months after Yalta, and Harry Truman, who had been vice president for eighty-two days, took his place. In March the Soviets had arrested sixteen leaders of the Polish underground after promising them safe conduct, a sign of how Stalin meant to treat Poland. At Potsdam in July the leaders met again, and Churchill was replaced halfway through by Clement Attlee after his party won the British election. Truman told Stalin that the United States had a powerful new weapon. Stalin showed little surprise, for Soviet spies had told him.

By 1946 Soviet-backed communists were taking power in Poland, Romania, Bulgaria, and Hungary, often through rigged elections, arrests, and threats. These countries became satellite statesSAT-uh-lyt STAYTA country that is officially independent but is actually controlled by a more powerful nation. See the glossary box.. In March 1946, in a speech at Fulton, Missouri, Winston Churchill, no longer prime minister, said that "an iron curtain has descended across the Continent." Stalin called him a warmonger. Yet the line he described was real. The two sides did not fight directly, so the contest was named the Cold War.

Each side feared the other, and each had reasons. The Soviets remembered the German invasion that had killed some twenty-seven million of their people, and they saw Western strength as a threat. The Americans saw a government that ruled by force at home and appeared to seek more power abroad. Neither side could easily tell defense from aggression. A single misreading could harden suspicion into hostility, and in these years many did.

CONTAINMENT

A young American diplomat in Moscow, George Kennan, argued in his famous Long Telegram, which you will read, that the Soviet leaders held a view of the world in which conflict with the West was permanent. He recommended patient resistance wherever Soviet power pressed outward. This became the policy of containmentkun-TAYN-muntThe American policy of stopping the spread of Soviet communism to new countries without going to war with the Soviet Union itself. See the glossary box..

The telegram ran to some eight thousand words, and it was read closely in Washington. Kennan argued that the Soviet rulers needed the picture of a hostile outside world to justify their harsh rule at home, so that friendly gestures from the West would not change them. At the same time he thought Soviet power was patient and cautious, and that it would give way when it met firm resistance. A year later, in an article that appeared without his name, he gave the policy its label.

The test came in 1947. Britain, exhausted, announced that it could no longer support the government of Greece, which was fighting a communist-led rebellion, or that of Turkey, which was under Soviet pressure. President Harry Truman asked Congress for aid. His speech, the Truman Doctrine, promised American support to free peoples resisting pressure from armed minorities or outside forces. It marked a turn: for the first time in peacetime, the United States took on lasting commitments in Europe.

Congress voted about four hundred million dollars in aid to Greece and Turkey in May 1947. With American arms and advisers, the Greek government defeated the rebels in 1949. It was the first time the new policy was tested, and it left the question of where such a policy should stop. Greece and Turkey lay at the edge of Soviet power, and nobody yet knew how far the commitment would reach.

Next came economic help. Western Europe lay in ruins, and hunger and poverty fed the appeal of communist parties in France and Italy. Secretary of State George Marshall proposed in June 1947 that America help rebuild Europe. Between 1948 and 1952 the Marshall Plan sent about thirteen billion dollars to sixteen countries. Stalin refused the aid for his own sphere and forced the satellite states to refuse as well. Western Europe recovered, and its trade and prosperity grew.

The offer was made to all of Europe, the Soviet Union included. At a conference in Paris in July 1947 the Soviet foreign minister, Molotov, walked out. Czechoslovakia, still a democracy, had accepted an invitation to attend, and Stalin ordered its government to withdraw. In February 1948 the Czech communists, backed by the police and by pressure from Moscow, seized power. In March the foreign minister, Jan Masaryk, the son of the country's founding president, was found dead beneath a window of his ministry. Whether he fell or was thrown has never been settled. The coup alarmed the West, which had counted Czechoslovakia among the countries that might remain free.

BERLIN AND THE TWO GERMANYS

Germany had been divided into four zones, and so had its capital, Berlin, which lay deep inside the Soviet zone. In 1948 the Western powers combined their zones and introduced a new currency. Stalin saw this as a threat and, in June, cut off all road, rail, and canal traffic to West Berlin, hoping to starve the city or force the West out. Truman refused to give way, but he did not want to fight a ground war. The answer was the Berlin Airlift. For nearly a year, American and British planes landed every few minutes, carrying coal, food, and medicine to two million people. Stalin ended the blockade in May 1949.

The airlift had to be improvised. General Lucius Clay, the American commander in Germany, first favored sending an armed convoy through the Soviet zone, but Truman would not risk a fight. West Berlin needed some 4,500 tons of supplies a day, and at the peak a plane landed about once every minute. A young American pilot, Gail Halvorsen, began dropping candy to the children who gathered near the airfield, tied to tiny parachutes made from handkerchiefs. West Berliners, whose city had been an enemy capital four years earlier, now came to see Americans as protectors.

The result was two German states. West Germany, a democratic republic, was founded in May 1949, and the Soviets created communist East Germany in October. In April 1949 the United States, Canada, and ten European nations formed the North Atlantic Treaty Organization, or NATO, a pledge that an attack on one was an attack on all. The Soviets formed the Warsaw Pact in 1955 with their satellites. That same year, Europe's division was complete.

THE ARMS RACE

In 1949 the Soviet Union tested its first atomic bomb, years sooner than American experts had expected, with help from spies who had stolen American secrets. Both sides soon built hydrogen bombs, many times more powerful, and long-range missiles to carry them. Fear of nuclear war kept the peace in Europe, for neither side could attack without being destroyed in return. This was deterrencedih-TER-untsKeeping an enemy from attacking by making clear that the cost of an attack would be too great. See the glossary box.. It was called mutual assured destruction, an acronym that spelled MAD. Yet the contest kept spreading to other parts of the world. In 1950 communist North Korea, with Stalin's approval and Chinese help, invaded South Korea. American-led United Nations forces fought for three years, and the war ended in 1953 close to where it had begun.

The first American hydrogen bomb was exploded in 1952, and the Soviets followed within a year. In 1957 the Soviet Union launched Sputnik, the first artificial satellite, which showed that it had a rocket able to cross continents and shocked Americans. In Korea, the Soviet Union had been boycotting the UN Security Council that summer, so it could not veto the decision to send forces. After a landing at Inchon in September 1950, General Douglas MacArthur drove the invaders back, but when UN troops neared China's border, Chinese armies entered the war and pushed them south. In April 1951 Truman removed MacArthur after he publicly challenged the policy of limiting the war. About 36,000 Americans died in Korea, and millions of Koreans.

The war changed American policy at home. A secret report of 1950, known as NSC-68, urged a large build-up of conventional and nuclear forces, and after the fighting began the defense budget roughly tripled. Fear reached into American life as well. In 1950 Senator Joseph McCarthy charged that communists had slipped into the State Department, and for several years suspicion ruined careers, often with little proof.

THE WALL AND THE BRINK

By 1961 about three million East Germans, many of them young and skilled, had left for the West, most of them through Berlin. In August the East German government built a wall of concrete and barbed wire across the city. Its guards were told to shoot those who tried to cross. President John Kennedy protested but did not send troops, judging that war was too high a price. The Berlin Wall became the best-known symbol of the division.

Khrushchev, who had demanded in 1958 that the Western powers leave Berlin, tested the young Kennedy at a summit in Vienna in June 1961. The Wall went up in the night of August 12 to 13, when soldiers unrolled barbed wire along the line, and only later was it rebuilt in concrete. In October American and Soviet tanks faced each other at the crossing point known as Checkpoint Charlie until both withdrew. At least 140 people were killed trying to cross the Wall in the years that followed.

The most dangerous moment came in October 1962. Fidel Castro, who had taken power in Cuba and turned it toward communism, accepted Soviet nuclear missiles on the island, only ninety miles from Florida. When American spy planes found them, Kennedy chose a naval quarantine instead of an air strike and demanded that the missiles be removed. For thirteen days the world held its breath. Soviet leader Nikita Khrushchev finally agreed to withdraw them, and in secret Kennedy agreed to remove American missiles from Turkey.

The crisis had a history. In April 1961 Kennedy had approved an invasion of Cuba by exiles trained by the CIA, and it failed at the Bay of Pigs. Khrushchev, who thought the young president weak, placed missiles in Cuba, and Castro feared another attack. An American U-2 spy plane photographed the sites on October 14. For a week Kennedy and a small group of advisers argued in secret, and some of the generals urged an air strike. On October 27, the most dangerous day, a U-2 was shot down over Cuba and its pilot killed, and Kennedy did not retaliate. That night his brother Robert told the Soviet ambassador privately about the missiles in Turkey, and the next morning Khrushchev announced the withdrawal.

The danger was greater than leaders knew. Accounts of the crisis say that on a Soviet submarine near Cuba, harassed by American ships, officers believed war had begun and one considered launching a nuclear torpedo. The decision required agreement from three officers, and one of them, Vasili Arkhipov, refused. Whether or not the story is exactly right, it shows how much rested on individual judgment at the edge of disaster. Afterward the two powers set up a telephone hotline, and in later years they signed arms limitation agreements.
