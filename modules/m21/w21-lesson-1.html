🎯 TODAY'S FOCUS

After 1492 the two halves of the world traded plants, animals, and diseases that had never met before. Today you will see how that exchange remade the world's diet and population, and how one sweet crop pulled the Atlantic into a new economy.

TWO WORLDS MEET

For many thousands of years, the Americas had developed apart from Europe, Africa, and Asia. They had their own crops, their own animals, and their own diseases. When Columbus crossed the ocean in 1492, he opened a door that would never close again. In 1972 the historian Alfred Crosby gave the result a name: the Columbian Exchangekuh-LUM-bee-un eks-CHAYNJThe movement of plants, animals, people, and diseases between the Americas and the rest of the world after 1492. See the glossary box.. It was not planned by any government. It happened because ships, sailors, and passengers carried living things with them, and some of those living things changed the world.

Think of Columbus's second voyage in 1493 as a floating farm. Seventeen ships crossed with well over a thousand men, and with them came horses, pigs, cattle, seeds, and cuttings. They also carried passengers that nobody had invited. Rats came out of the holds, and the seeds of European weeds rode in the hay and in the soil used to weigh the ships down. A common weed, the broad-leaved plantain, became known to Native Americans in the English colonies as the Englishman's foot, because it seemed to spring up wherever the settlers walked. Honeybees followed in later years, and Native Americans called them the white man's flies.

WHAT CROSSED THE OCEAN

Europeans brought horses, cattle, pigs, sheep, wheat, and grapes to the Americas. Horses changed life on the Great Plains, where peoples learned to ride and hunt on horseback. Cattle and pigs spread across the continent, because the land had room and no natural enemies for them.

Horses were weapons as well as animals. When Cortés landed on the Mexican coast in 1519, he brought about sixteen of them. Spanish writers later claimed that people who had never seen a horse took horse and rider for a single, terrifying creature, and the Spaniards were glad to let the belief spread. Horses did not win the conquest by themselves, because the allies and the diseases you read about earlier mattered far more. Still, a charge of mounted men on open ground was something no army in the Americas had ever faced.

The Spaniards guarded their horses closely and tried to keep them out of native hands. But horses were bred in the Spanish settlements of what is now New Mexico, and in 1680 the Pueblo peoples rose against Spain and drove the settlers out, leaving many animals behind. In the decades that followed, herds and the skills to manage them spread northward from one people to the next, by trade and by theft. Peoples such as the Comanche and the Lakota built new ways of life around the horse, following the buffalo herds across wide plains, and by the 1700s they were among the strongest powers on the continent.

In the other direction went crops that fed the world. Maize and potatoes, which grew well in poor soil, spread across Europe, Africa, and Asia. The potato in particular was a cheap source of calories, and it helped feed growing populations across northern Europe. Cassava, a starchy root, became a staple in parts of Africa. Tomatoes, beans, peppers, and cacao came as well, and so did tobacco, which Europeans quickly learned to smoke and to sell.

The most deadly passengers were invisible. Europeans carried smallpox, measles, and influenza. People in the Americas had never met these diseases, and they had no resistance. In the Caribbean the Taíno people, who had greeted Columbus, nearly vanished within two generations. On the mainland the population fell by a huge share over the next century, though historians still argue about the exact numbers. The Spaniards did not understand what was happening, and they did not intend it. Spanish labor demands added to the toll. By the 1500s, whole islands stood empty of the people who had once farmed them.

Why were the Americas so exposed? Many of Europe's worst diseases began in herd animals, and people caught them from the cattle, pigs, and fowl they lived beside. Europeans had kept such animals for thousands of years, and adults who had survived an outbreak as children were often protected from the next one. The Americas had few large herd animals, so the same sicknesses had never crossed over to the people who tended them. A European who had lived through smallpox was safe from it. In the Americas no one had, and it fell on the young and the old alike.

The diseases often ran ahead of the explorers. Smallpox that began in one village could travel along trade routes to a village no European had ever seen. You have already met this pattern, since smallpox struck Tenochtitlan in 1520 and probably reached the Inca lands before Pizarro did. A disease that sickens a whole village at once leaves no healthy nurses and no one to plant the crops, and the hunger that follows can kill more people than the first sickness. Few places had time to recover before the next wave arrived.

A NEW WORLD DIET

These changes did not only reach the people who first met them. The potato took a long time to win acceptance in Europe, since many people distrusted a food that grew underground. But it grew well in cold, wet climates, and a small plot could feed a family. In places such as Ireland and northern Germany, populations rose. In China, farmers planted maize and sweet potatoes on hillsides where rice would not grow, and these crops helped the population increase in the 1600s and 1700s.

Other American plants changed habits more than diets. Tobacco, which King James I of England disliked, became a huge business anyway. Chocolate, a drink the Aztecs had prized, became a fashion in Spain and then across Europe. Many of these goods were soon grown far from where they began. Cacao, for example, was planted in Africa and Asia. The world's farms were slowly becoming one.

Why was the potato so useful? An acre planted in potatoes gives more calories than an acre of wheat, and the crop grows in cold, wet ground where grain does poorly. The same fact carried a risk, because a people that depends on one crop is in trouble when it fails, and Ireland would learn that in the 1840s. Tobacco spread even faster. In 1612 John Rolfe, an English settler in Virginia, planted a sweet variety from the Caribbean, and within a few years Virginia was shipping tobacco to England. A colony that had nearly starved a few years earlier now lived by a single crop sold across the sea.

THE ROAD OF SUGAR

One of the plants that crossed the ocean had a long history already. Sugarcane began in the Pacific and was farmed in India. Arab traders carried it around the Mediterranean, and the Crusaders, whom you met earlier, brought a taste for it home. Sugar was rare and expensive, a luxury for the rich. In the 1400s the Portuguese planted cane on islands in the Atlantic, such as Madeira. On his second voyage in 1493 Columbus brought cane cuttings to the island of Hispaniola.

Sugar was hard to grow and harder to make. It needs heat, rain, and a great deal of land. The cane must be cut at exactly the right time and crushed within a day, or the juice spoils. The juice is then boiled in huge pots over fires that burn day and night. Mills and boiling houses cost a fortune. The result was a new kind of farm, the plantationplan-TAY-shunA large farm that grows one cash crop, such as sugar, for sale far away. See the glossary box., where hundreds of workers labored in gangs under one owner to produce a single crop for sale across the sea.

Follow the cane through the mill. Rollers turned by water, wind, or animals squeezed out the juice. The juice ran into a row of copper kettles, each hotter than the last, and workers skimmed it and added lime until it thickened. The thick syrup was poured into clay molds shaped like cones and left to drain. What dripped out was molasses. What stayed in the mold was a hard loaf of sugar, and the molasses could in turn be fermented and distilled into rum. Every step needed fuel, skilled hands, and long hours. The Portuguese had already learned this on the Atlantic islands, and on the island of São Tomé in the 1500s they put enslaved Africans to work in the cane fields. Other planters took notice.

Sugar also devoured land and fuel. The boiling houses burned wood by the cartload, and a cane field wears out the soil when it is planted year after year. On Barbados, which had been thickly wooded when the English arrived, most of the forest was cleared within a few decades. Once the juice had been pressed out, the dry stalks of the crushed cane were burned to keep the kettles hot, but there was never enough of them. A sugar island that had been green and wooded became an island of cane fields.

The Dutch occupation lasted longer than a raid. Their governor in Brazil, Johan Maurits, rebuilt the city of Recife with canals and bridges. The Dutch also captured Portuguese forts in West Africa and Angola, so that they could supply their own plantations with enslaved workers. When they were driven out of Brazil, they took their knowledge of the sugar business with them, and they went looking for new places to use it.

Elsewhere meant the Caribbean. English settlers had landed on the island of Barbados in 1627 and tried tobacco, but the profits were small. In the 1640s they turned to sugar, and the island changed almost at once. The work was so brutal and the diseases so deadly that many died before their contracts ended. England took Jamaica from Spain in 1655, and France built the colony of Saint-Domingue on the western half of Hispaniola. Each of these colonies followed the same path.

Sugar made Barbados one of the most crowded and wealthiest places in the English world, and it pushed people out. A man who finished his contract and hoped for a small farm found the best land already planted in cane by large owners. Many left for other colonies. In 1670 planters from Barbados helped found the colony of Carolina on the mainland, and they brought with them their ideas about how to organize labor.

WHY SUGAR WON

Europeans soon wanted all the sugar they could get. It sweetened the tea and coffee and chocolate that were becoming popular drinks, and it turned from a luxury into an everyday food. Planters, merchants, and governments all saw profit in this, and profit shaped what each of them did. Nobody in this chain needed a grand theory. They needed workers, and plenty of them. Look at how different this is from the Spanish conquests you read about earlier. There, the prize was gold and silver that could be taken. Here, the prize was a crop that had to be planted, cut, crushed, and boiled again and again, year after year. A farm like that runs on labor more than on any other resource.

The numbers show how quickly a luxury became a habit. By one estimate, the average English person ate about four pounds of sugar a year around 1700 and about eighteen pounds by 1800. Every new drinker of tea and coffee was another customer for the plantations. The planters faced arithmetic of their own. An indentured servant's contract ended after a few years, and a freed servant wanted land of his own, which on a small island meant land the planters wanted for cane. As the island filled and wages in England improved, fewer men were willing to sign.

Sugar had one more advantage over most crops. It was worth a great deal for its weight, and it kept for months in barrels, so a ship's hold full of it could pay for a whole voyage. Raw sugar went to refineries in port cities such as London and Amsterdam, which made fortunes for merchants at home and gave governments a reason to protect the trade.

That was the problem. Native populations had collapsed in the Caribbean. Indentured servants were too few and died too fast, and as English conditions improved, fewer people were willing to volunteer. The planters needed a labor supply that would not run out, and they were willing to buy it. In the next lesson you will see where it came from, and the terrible journey that brought it across the sea.
