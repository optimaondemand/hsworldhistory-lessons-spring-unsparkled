1Paragraph 1. Napoleon Bonaparte was born in 1769 on the island of Corsica, a year after France bought it from Genoa. His family belonged to the minor nobility, and at home they spoke Italian. When he won a place at a French military school at the age of nine, the other boys mocked his accent. He answered with hard work. He was especially good at mathematics, which suited the artillery, and he read widely, including Plutarch's Lives, until the ancient heroes became his models. At sixteen he held an officer's commission. In the old royal army, though, promotion went mostly to men of great family and good connections, and a young man from a poor island with no friends at court could expect to wait a very long time. When the Revolution came, the old rules that kept officers of humble birth from rising no longer held.

2Paragraph 2. Corsica itself pulled at him. His family had once fought beside the island's patriot leader, Pasquale Paoli, and as a young officer Napoleon dreamed of serving Corsica more than France. He spent long leaves there and took a hand in its politics. Then the Revolution split the island. In 1793 Paoli broke with the government in Paris, and Napoleon, who had sided with the Republic, found that he and his family were now on the wrong side at home. The Bonapartes fled to the mainland with little more than they could carry. From then on his future lay in France.

3Paragraph 3. His first chance came in 1793, when British ships held the port of Toulon, which French royalists had handed over to them. The army besieging the town had no plan that worked. Napoleon, a captain of artillery, saw that the harbor was ringed by hills, and that guns placed on one height above the roadstead would make it too dangerous for the fleet to stay. He pressed the plan on his superiors until they accepted it, and the British sailed away. At twenty-four he was a general.

4Paragraph 4. His rise nearly ended the next summer. After Robespierre fell in July 1794, Napoleon was arrested for a short time because he had been friendly with Robespierre's younger brother. He was freed, but he spent the following year without a command, short of money, and looking for any post that would have him. Then, in October 1795, a royalist uprising threatened the Convention in Paris. The man in charge of the defense, Paul Barras, remembered Napoleon from Toulon and called him in the night before. Napoleon sent a young cavalry officer, Joachim Murat, galloping across the city to bring up cannon before the crowd could seize them. Napoleon fired cannon into the crowd and ended the rising in an afternoon. The Directory, grateful, gave him command of the army in Italy. Within months he had married Josephine de Beauharnais, a widow whose first husband had died on the guillotine and who moved in Barras's circle. Two days later he left for the front.

5Paragraph 5. In 1796 he took over a ragged army whose soldiers were hungry, badly paid, and in some cases without shoes. He told them they would find glory and wealth if they followed him. The enemy armies, one from Austria and one from the kingdom of Piedmont, stood side by side but were poorly joined. He moved fast, struck at the place where they met, and defeated them one by one. His men marched light and lived on what they could take from the countryside instead of waiting for wagons, so they often arrived before the enemy thought they could. Piedmont was out of the war in weeks. Then came a series of victories at Lodi, Arcole, and Rivoli. At Lodi his men rushed a narrow bridge under cannon fire.

6Paragraph 6. In 1797 he forced Austria to sign a peace at Campo Formio, which gave France the Austrian Netherlands, and he negotiated the terms himself. The Directory in Paris, which had expected only a sideshow in Italy, now found that its general was making treaties on his own and sending home gold and famous paintings and statues from Italian cities. He was already acting like a ruler. Newspapers he supported, some of them founded for his own army, made him famous in Paris.

7Paragraph 7. Notice what kind of leader he was. He slept little, remembered the names of soldiers, and worked out the details of supply and marching himself. His men followed him because he won, and because he promised that a soldier could rise as far as his talent would carry him. Many of his marshals had been sergeants or sons of innkeepers. That promise tied the army to him more than it tied it to any government.

8Paragraph 8. Paris, meanwhile, had a problem. A general this popular was a danger to a government this weak. When Napoleon proposed an expedition to Egypt, the Directors agreed, partly because it would put him far away. The plan was to seize a rich province and threaten Britain's road to India, striking at an enemy France could not reach across the Channel. He sailed in 1798 with an army and, unusually, with more than a hundred scholars, engineers, and artists.
