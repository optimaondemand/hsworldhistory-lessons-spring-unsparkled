1Paragraph 1. Now he could build. He founded the Bank of France to steady the currency, so that the government could borrow and pay its bills. He appointed a prefect to govern each department, a system that echoed the intendants of Louis XIV. These officials took orders from Paris, which is how a government in the capital could collect taxes and call up soldiers in every corner of France. He started public high schools called lycées, run by the state to train the officials and officers the country needed. And he made peace with the Church through a Concordat with Pope Pius VII in 1801. The Revolution had seized Church lands and forced priests to swear an oath to the state, and French Catholics had been divided. The Concordat recognized Catholicism as the faith of most French people, let the government nominate bishops, and paid the clergy from the treasury. But it kept the Church lands in the hands of those who had bought them.

2Paragraph 2. His most lasting work was the Napoleonic Code of 1804, a single set of laws for the nation. In the old France the law had differed from province to province, and a family dispute could turn on where you happened to live. A commission of lawyers drew on Roman law and the older customs, and Napoleon sat in on many of its sessions himself. The Code kept the great gains of 1789: equality before the law, an end to feudal privilege, protection of property, and religious freedom. But it also gave husbands wide power over wives and children, and a married woman could not sign a contract without her husband's consent.

3Paragraph 3. Careers were open to talent, and the new Legion of Honor, founded in 1802, rewarded merit rather than birth. Yet this was also a police state. Newspapers were censored, and soon only a handful of the Paris papers were left. A secret police under the minister Joseph Fouché watched for plots, and elections became formalities.

4Paragraph 4. Royalist plots against his life gave his supporters an argument. In December 1800 a bomb exploded in a Paris street just after his carriage passed, on the way to the opera. If one bullet could end the regime, they said, France needed a government that would outlast one man. In 1802 another plebiscite made him consul for life. In March 1804, after another royalist plot was uncovered, French soldiers crossed the Rhine into the German state of Baden, seized a young Bourbon prince, the Duke of Enghien, and shot him after a hasty trial. The prince had taken no part in the plot. Within weeks the Senate asked Napoleon to take the title of emperor, so that the succession would be settled and no royalist could gain by killing him. In 1804 a third plebiscite approved a new title for him, Emperor of the French.

5Paragraph 5. At his coronation in Notre-Dame, on December 2, 1804, Pope Pius VII was present, but Napoleon placed the crown on his own head. He wore a wreath of golden laurel leaves, like a Roman emperor, and then crowned Josephine himself. The republic of virtue was gone. It had been replaced by a regime that depended on the will and talent of one man, which is what Polybius would have expected to see. The Romans had seen their own republic end in the same way, with Caesar.

6Paragraph 6. War with Britain returned in 1803. Napoleon gathered an army at Boulogne and planned to invade England but first needed control of the Channel. On October 21, 1805, Nelson attacked the combined French and Spanish fleet off Cape Trafalgar in two columns, cutting through the enemy line instead of sailing parallel to it. He destroyed the enemy fleet and died in the moment of victory, shot by a sharpshooter on a French ship. Britain was safe at sea for the rest of the war.

7Paragraph 7. On land, however, Napoleon was unstoppable. Austria and Russia had joined Britain against him that year, and he swung his army from the Channel coast to the Danube in a matter of weeks. Raising armies by conscription and living off the lands he conquered, he beat the Austrians and Russians at Austerlitz on December 2, 1805, perhaps his greatest victory. Austerlitz was won by a trap. He let the enemy believe he was weak, gave up the high ground at the center, and waited. The Allies came down to crush what looked like his exposed right flank. Then his troops stormed the thinned center and split their army in two. Austria made peace within weeks.

8Paragraph 8. In 1806 he crushed Prussia at Jena, and within weeks he rode into Berlin. In 1807, at Tilsit, the Russian tsar Alexander I met him on a raft moored in the Niemen River, made peace, and agreed to cooperate with him.

9Paragraph 9. By 1807 Napoleon was master of most of Europe. He made his brothers kings: Joseph in Naples, Louis in Holland, and Jérôme in Westphalia. The old Holy Roman Empire was dissolved in 1806. Was he the heir of the Revolution, spreading its laws and its equality? Or had he become its gravedigger? Next lesson you will see how that question was answered.
