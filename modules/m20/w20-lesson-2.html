🎯 TODAY'S FOCUS

A small republic with no king, few natural resources, and a damp climate became the richest country in Europe. Today you will see how Dutch merchants did it, and what it cost.

A CITY BUILT ON MUD

Amsterdam sat on soft ground at the mouth of a small river. Its builders had to drive thousands of wooden piles into the mud to hold up a single house. It began as a fishing town, but in 1585 Spain captured Antwerp and closed the river that served it. Merchants, bankers, and skilled craftsmen fled north by the thousands, and many of them settled in Amsterdam. Within a few decades the city had tripled in size. It had gained experienced traders almost overnight, and it knew what to do with them.

The city planned for its growth. From about 1613 it dug a ring of new canals around the old center and sold the land along them to merchants. The merchants built tall, narrow houses, partly because the city taxed a house by the width of its front. Goods came up from the boats on the canal by a beam and hook at the top of the gable and went into warehouse space under the roof. A merchant lived above his stock and watched it come in.

SHIPS AND FREIGHT

The Dutch had little land, so they lived off the sea. They caught and salted herring, and they sailed to the Baltic to bring back grain, timber, and tar. This grain trade was so large that the Dutch called it the mother of all trades. A new kind of ship made it cheaper. The Dutch called it the fluytFLOYTA Dutch word for a broad, cheap cargo ship that needed only a small crew. See the glossary box.. It was broad, plain, and cheap to build, and it needed fewer sailors than rival ships. The Dutch used it to haul goods for other nations as well as their own. Soon they had the largest merchant fleet in Europe, and a Dutch captain would carry your cargo for less than your own countrymen would.

Each of these trades rested on a small piece of method. Dutch herring boats gutted and salted the catch on board, so that it kept for months and could be sold far inland and to Catholic countries on fast days. The fluytFLOYTA Dutch word for a broad, cheap cargo ship that needed only a small crew. See the glossary box. was designed around a single question: how can a ship carry the most cargo for the lowest cost? It had a deep, rounded hold and few guns, and its sails were rigged with pulleys that let a small crew do the work of a larger one. Wind-driven sawmills near Amsterdam cut the timber for these ships far faster than men could with hand saws. Every Baltic voyage passed through a narrow strait where the Danish king collected a toll, and Dutch ships made up most of the traffic. A ship that carries more per sailor can charge less and still make a profit, and the rivals who built bigger, armed ships for show found they could not match the price.

A NATION OF READERS

The Dutch were also unusually well educated. Many ordinary townspeople could read and do arithmetic, and the Republic's printers were bold. Amsterdam publishers produced maps, atlases, and books that were banned in other countries. Willem Blaeu's maps guided Dutch captains around the world. Thinkers who were not safe at home came to live in Dutch cities. The French philosopher René Descartes spent most of twenty years in the Republic, and Baruch Spinoza grew up in Amsterdam's Jewish community.

A printer in a country that did not hang authors could print whatever he judged would sell. Galileo's last great book, which the Church would not allow to appear in Italy, was printed in Leiden in 1638 by the Elzevir family. Descartes's Discourse on Method had come out in Leiden the year before. The English philosopher John Locke lived in the Republic for several years in the 1680s, keeping out of reach of the English king's agents. City magistrates usually left the booksellers alone, because the books made money and the fame was good for the city.

The lawyer Hugo Grotius gave the Republic's merchants an argument as well as a map. In 1609 he published a pamphlet claiming that the sea was open to the ships of every nation, so that Portugal had no right to shut the Asian trade to the Dutch. The VOC liked that argument. It did not always apply it to itself. Once its own ships had control of a trade, the company often shut out competitors.

THE EAST INDIA COMPANY

The most valuable cargo in the world was spice. Pepper, cloves, nutmeg, and cinnamon grew in Asia, and the Portuguese had controlled that trade for a century. In the 1590s Dutch merchants sent their own fleets to Asia. They soon found themselves competing against one another and driving up the price of spices at the source. In 1602 the government of the Republic brought them together. The result was the Dutch East India Company, known by its Dutch initials, the VOC.

The VOC was a joint-stock companyjoint-STOK KUM-puh-neeA business owned by many investors who each buy shares. Each owner risks only the money he or she put in. See the glossary box.. Anyone with money, from a rich merchant to a widow with some savings, could buy a share. If the voyages failed, an investor lost only what he or she had put in. Shares could be resold, and that was the clever part. A buyer no longer had to wait years for a fleet to return. He could sell his share to someone else in the meantime. The government also gave the company a twenty-one-year monopolymuh-NOP-uh-leeThe sole right to sell a good or run a trade, so that no one else is allowed to compete. See the glossary box. on Dutch trade east of the Cape of Good Hope. In 1609 the city opened a public bank to hold merchants' deposits and settle their accounts.

A board of seventeen directors steered the company. Each year its ships sailed round the southern tip of Africa to Java, carrying silver and bringing back pepper, cloves, nutmeg, and cloth. The voyage was deadly. Scurvy and fever killed many sailors, and a large share of the men who went east never came home. At home the shares changed hands in a market that soon had its own building, and some traders even bet that the price of shares would fall, a practice the authorities tried to ban. The bank of 1609 solved a quieter problem. Merchants arrived with coins from many countries, and many of them were worn or clipped. The bank weighed them, gave each depositor a credit of the right value, and moved credit from one account to another without moving a single coin. Merchants could settle a debt with a stroke of the pen and trust that the number meant what it said.

THE SPICE ISLANDS

The company was a trading firm, but it also had soldiers, forts, and warships, and it behaved like a government. In 1619 it founded a headquarters at Batavia, on the island of Java. In 1621 its governor-general, Jan Pieterszoon Coen, attacked the Banda Islands, the main source of nutmeg in the world. The islanders had refused to sell only to the Dutch. Coen's forces killed or drove out or enslaved most of the people there and brought in enslaved laborers to work the nutmeg groves. The monopoly was enforced by arms as well as by contract.

Batavia stood on the north coast of Java on the site of a town the Dutch had burned, and its canals and tall houses were laid out like those of Amsterdam. Nutmeg grew in only a few small islands, and the company meant to buy all of it at its own price. The Bandanese also sold to English and other traders, and Coen treated that as a broken contract. After the conquest the company gave the nutmeg groves to its own employees, who worked them with enslaved labor, and the nutmeg sold in Europe for many times what it cost to produce. The tiny island of Run shows how far the contest went. England held a claim to it, and in 1667 a treaty confirmed Dutch control. In the same treaty the Dutch gave up New Netherland, their own colony on the coast of North America, which became New York.

TOLERANCE AND REGENTS

The Dutch also became known for a practical kind of toleration. The Union of Utrecht had promised that no one would be questioned about his beliefs. Jews who had fled Spain and Portugal settled in Amsterdam and built a synagogue. French Protestants arrived after them. Catholics were allowed to worship quietly in hidden churches, though not in public. It was not equality. Only members of the Calvinist church held high office. But it was a tolerance with good business sense in it, and skilled people came to the country as a result.

Toleration worked mostly because of the town magistrates. The Calvinist church was the official church, and its ministers often demanded that the law be enforced against Catholics and others. The magistrates usually answered with fines, or looked the other way, because they did not want to drive away taxpaying neighbors. A small group of English separatists, who had left England so they could worship in their own way, lived in the city of Leiden for about eleven years. Some of them later sailed to America on the Mayflower.

The Republic had no king. Each town was run by a small circle of wealthy merchant families called regents. They governed through councils, and they usually preferred peace and low risks. The stadtholderSTAD-hole-derIn the Low Countries, a governor who ruled a province in the name of a king or ruling council. See the glossary box., the office William of Orange had once held, was a military command, and its holders often rivaled the regents. In 1618 the stadtholder Maurice quarreled with the leading statesman of Holland, Johan van Oldenbarnevelt, over religion and the war with Spain. The next year Oldenbarnevelt was executed for treason. A republic of merchants could be ruthless to its own leaders.

The religious quarrel was about a hard question in Calvinist teaching: whether God has already chosen who will be saved, or whether a person's own choice counts for something. Oldenbarnevelt backed the side that gave free choice more room, and he wanted each province to settle the dispute for itself. Maurice backed the strict side, which wanted a national church and one national decision. When towns in Holland raised their own hired guards, Maurice marched in with the army and disarmed them. Oldenbarnevelt, now seventy-one years old, was arrested. A special court convicted him, and he was beheaded in The Hague in May 1619. Grotius, the lawyer you met earlier in this lesson, was sentenced to life in prison and escaped in 1621 by hiding in a chest said to have held books.

    FREE, OR JUST RICH?

    By the middle of the century Dutch painters such as Rembrandt were filling the walls of merchant houses with portraits. The Republic had solved its first-order problems better than any state of its day. It kept order, raised taxes people would pay, and defended itself with a navy. Its habits of thrift, hard work, and trust in contracts helped create wealth that a king could not command. Foreign visitors remarked that Dutch merchants dressed plainly, kept honest weights, and paid their debts, and that a handshake in Amsterdam counted for a great deal.

    Look at what that good name could buy. The Dutch cities could borrow money at lower interest than the king of Spain, who had defaulted again and again, because lenders trusted them to repay and often lived in the same towns as the borrowers. Not every Dutch habit was sober. In the 1630s the prices of rare tulip bulbs rose wildly for a few winters and then collapsed. Meanwhile the citizen militias of Amsterdam paid painters to show them as defenders of the city, and Rembrandt's Night Watch of 1642 was one such commission.

    But the classical thinkers you have met would ask a harder question. Polybius and Machiavelli feared that riches could soften a people and turn citizens into customers. Was the Republic held together by common love of country, or only by profit? And what part did the labor of unfree workers, in the Spice Islands and soon in the Atlantic, play in that wealth? The Dutch West India Company, founded in 1621, would carry thousands of enslaved Africans to the Americas. That brings us to next week.
